A practical guide to using evidence cards in realist and co-design workshops

What are If/Then cards?

If/Then cards bring academic evidence into participatory workshops in the form of a card game. They were developed as a cross-over method blending realist synthesis with co-design in the NIHR-funded Function First project (NIHR17/45/22).

Each card carries one statement from a CMO (Context–Mechanism–Outcome) set drawn from the academic literature. The statements are academic evidence, and they come in three types:

If
describes the context: “if this set of conditions exists…”
Then
describes the outcome: “…then this will be the outcome.”
Because
describes the mechanism: how or why those conditions lead to that outcome: “…because…”

Cards turn the first-pass “long list” of potential If, Then and Because statements into something a workshop can engage with. They help participants to:

  • get to know each other and each other’s experience and expertise;
  • become familiar with the academic evidence quickly;
  • start relating the evidence to their own experiences and understanding.

This guide describes when and how to use the cards in a realist or realist-co-design study, where they may not work so well, and where to find more on how they have been developed and used.

When to use them

Realist reviews and syntheses are iterative:

  1. Examine the literature for extractable data (Contexts, Mechanisms and Outcomes) and add them to a long list in a spreadsheet.
  2. In each later iteration, refine and condense the list: group, collapse and combine CMOs, and occasionally add new theoretical areas based on expert feedback.
  3. Create a new sheet in the master spreadsheet for each iteration, rather than editing the original long list.
Our advice: the earlier, the better

The cards can be generated at any iteration. Involving wider expertise through the card games, from the first iteration makes you more likely to spot missing theoretical areas sooner, and to make meaningful refinements at each stage.

How to use them

The cards are a game that encourages turn-taking and dialogue. Use small groups of no more than six with mixed stakeholders, for example a health researcher, a health professional and a health service user.

Setting up

  • Lay out two decks: one of If cards and one of Then cards.
  • Shuffle both decks.

Version 1: Pair and test

  1. The player draws one card from each deck and reads both aloud.
  2. They decide whether the two statements are related: would this set of conditions (If) cause this outcome (Then)?
  3. They announce the decision, explain their reasoning and ask whether others agree.
  4. Disagreement is welcome. Players debate their explanations for a few minutes.
  5. The group decides. Connected: record the Because statement the group agrees on. Unconnected: discard the cards.
  6. Play passes to the next player, who repeats the process.

Version 2: Find the match

  1. Player 1 draws an If card and reads it aloud.
  2. They keep drawing and reading out Then cards until they find the one they feel is the best match.
  3. As in Version 1, they explain their choice, and others can disagree or offer alternative explanations.
  4. Once a best match is found, play passes to Player 2.

Why it works

The Because statements come from players’ own experience and expertise, while the cards come from the literature. The process therefore combines academic and experiential evidence by design.

When not to use them

We have no strict criteria, only advice, and it comes with a caveat: the evidence behind it is limited. In the applications we have run, a couple of times the cards did not achieve their intended results. The main difference was the type of evidence in the literature:

  • Worked well: evidence based on concrete, tried-and-tested interventions, where specific conditions and outcomes could be stated.
  • Worked less well: evidence that was abstract, theoretical or philosophical, where conditions and outcomes were vaguer.

Limitations

The game has not been used across a wide range of applications. Before committing to it as a participatory method in a study, try it with relevant data from that study on a small group of friendly people.
